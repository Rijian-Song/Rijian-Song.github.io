## Keeping the material and the interface intact

A tissue adhesive can fail within the hydrogel or detach from the tissue. My work treats these failure modes together, connecting interfacial chemistry with the ability of the bulk network to carry and dissipate load.

Our **cohesion–adhesion balance** study used a PVA/alginate double network to show how adjusting the contributions of the two networks improved tissue adhesion. In more recent work, cationic injectable networks made from thiolated HA and acrylate-functionalised hyperbranched PEG combined wet-tissue sealing with enhanced red blood cell retention under the tested conditions.

## Questions guiding this direction

- **Formation of contact:** How do gelation time, charge density, and tissue surface conditions affect spreading, attachment, and washout before the network has formed?
- **Failure under use:** Does the material fail by interfacial separation or cohesive tearing, and how does that change under fluid exposure and repeated movement?
- **Attachment over time:** Can adhesion persist through swelling and early degradation, then diminish in a way that supports tissue remodelling or atraumatic removal?

A priority for future work is **interfacial fatigue**. Recent bioadhesion research shows why durability under cyclic loading needs its own assessment alongside single-load adhesion measurements. I want to apply that distinction to injectable systems, with wet conditioning and loading histories chosen for the intended tissue interface.
